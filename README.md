# The Quarry

**A living inventory of the rocks, minerals, and crystals in a household, built from photos taken on a phone.**

The Quarry is where a rock collection gets catalogued, named, and explained. Photograph a geode from a gem show, a handful of agates from a beach, or a crystal point from a gift shop, and the Quarry suggests what it is, asks for a couple of kitchen-table tests to tell the lookalikes apart, adds it to the collection once a person confirms the name, and pulls together what is known about the mineral: its formula, its hardness, its crystal system, where it is found. Over time the egg cartons, tackle boxes, and windowsills become a collection a person actually wants to open, and every specimen gets a label that can be scanned.

It is being built for the rock hounds in one family, who come home from beaches, creek beds, road cuts, and gem shows with their pockets full. A collection like that outgrows memory fast: which drawer the fluorite is in, where the geode came from, whether anyone ever worked out what the green one is. The Quarry is the version that remembers.

It also teaches. The person building it could not tell quartz from calcite, which makes a useful test. Every mineralogical term the app shows carries a small info mark that explains it in plain language, with an example from the person's own collection when there is one. Nobody should have to leave the app to understand the app.

> **Status:** Phase 1 is built and Phase 2 is well along. The Quarry runs at the house on orchid, beside the Aviary and the Conservatory, for phones on the house Wi-Fi at `https://quarry.lan`. A rock goes in as up to five photos, two models name it, the owner confirms with a tap, and each stone gets a page with what Mindat and Wikipedia know about its mineral. The mineral pages, the labels, and the field stations are still to come. This README is the design brief; how the code is built is in the [technical guide](docs/guide/README.md).

## Lineage

The Quarry is the fifth app in a family of home-scale projects, and it is being built alongside the fourth:

- **[The Conservatory](https://github.com/reclinerhead/conservatory)** is the twin. Same idea, same shape, for plants: photos go in, a worker on the household's station asks an identifier, candidates come out, the owner confirms, an enrichment pass builds each species page, and a hub gathers every household into one family view. The Quarry follows that design almost step for step. It differs at one point, the identifier, because rocks have no Pl@ntNet.
- **[The Aviary](https://github.com/reclinerhead/aviary)** is the template both twins follow: a purpose-built identifier, an enrichment pass from Wikipedia, a page per species, field stations at family members' homes, and a community hub. The Quarry runs on the same boxes.
- **[Hearth](https://github.com/reclinerhead/hearth)** contributes the uploader: a phone-first capture flow where a photo becomes a structured record, with browser-side compression so uploads survive a weak connection.
- **Échoes**, a private family archive, contributes the identification step. Its photo wizard shows suggestion pills, lets the person type a name instead, saves every confirmation immediately, and makes every mis-tap reversible. Those rules were learned with older family members as the users, and they carry over unchanged.

## What it does

- **Add a rock from its photos.** Put one rock together from up to five photos, taken in the app or chosen from the camera roll (a close-up in daylight, one with a coin beside it for scale, one of any label or tag), add a note about where it came from, and send it. Each rock, with all its photos, is one identification job. The rocks sent so far wait in a strip at the top of the page, which turns amber once Jasper, the worker, has a guess ready.
- **Name it from two opinions.** Jasper sends the photos and the note to two vision language models and shows their answer as pills: **probably** Galena when they agree, **could be** Pyrite or Chalcopyrite when they don't. A "Why?" under each pill lists what the models saw. The owner taps a pill, picks a name already in the collection, or types the name from the dealer's label, then says how they know. A test is offered when one would tell the candidates apart.
- **Keep the collection.** The dashboard opens with what changed today ("Your Fluorite filled slot 4 on the Mohs scale, and calcium and fluorine are now lit on your periodic table."), the strip of stones waiting for a name, and every stone as a card, with search, filters (crystals, rocks and fossils, tumbled and polished; found or bought), and sorting. Beside it are the newest additions, the counts at a glance, the household's Mohs scale, and where the stones live.
- **A page for every stone.** Its photos, with a main photo the owner chooses and a full-size view with zoom. Its specimen label, edited in place: where it came from, how and when, where it lives now, the other minerals on it, its form, and its notes. Its kitchen-table tests, and how it was named. Then what is known about its mineral: "About" from Wikipedia; the property card from Mindat (formula, Mohs hardness, crystal system, luster, streak, cleavage, specific gravity, fluorescence); where Mindat reports it across the USA and in Michigan's counties; the elements it is made of; and where it sits on the Mohs scale.
- **Light up the periodic table.** Every element in a named mineral on any stone lights up on a periodic table, and tapping one lists the stones that carry it. It is a join over the Mindat records, not a model call.
- **Fill the Mohs scale.** The ten reference minerals, talc through diamond, are a set to collect. A fluorite, or anything Mindat files under fluorite, fills slot 4. A hardness alone never fills a slot: galena at 2.5 fills nothing.
- **Plan the show cases.** For the club's yearly rock show: lay out each display case from the collection, in order, by tapping on a phone or dragging on a laptop, and put a stone in a case from its own page. Each stone carries its number in the case, so "7 goes next to 8" reads the same on any screen at the hall.
- **Explain itself.** Variety, matrix, form, made of, Mohs hardness, streak, luster, cleavage, crystal system, specific gravity, mineral class, rock, element, locality: every term on the screen carries an info mark with a two- or three-sentence note.

## Still to come

- **Know the minerals.** A page per mineral as well as per stone: the property card, the Wikipedia summary and reference image, every specimen of it in the household, and its relatives in the collection, meaning other members of the same mineral group. Short handling notes for each mineral (below). A glossary whose notes use the household's own stones as the example: "Mohs 7: harder than a steel knife, which is why the amethyst from the gift shop scratched the glass."
- **Label the specimens.** Collectors already keep a paper label in each specimen's box (name, locality, date, collector) and a catalog number dabbed on the stone itself. The Quarry will assign the numbers and print the labels on sheets, each with a QR code that opens the specimen's page, so a phone camera can tell anyone what a stone is and where it came from ([#43](https://github.com/reclinerhead/quarry/issues/43)). A show case will print its show cards and labels in the case's order ([#82](https://github.com/reclinerhead/quarry/issues/82)). A short provenance log will record what happened to each stone: found, bought, gifted, traded, cleaned, moved, given away. A specimen that came out of a grandparent's collection keeps that history.
- **Find places to look.** A hand-picked list of Michigan places to look for stones, nearest first, with what the rules let a person take at each, and the household's own places with notes ([#59](https://github.com/reclinerhead/quarry/issues/59)).
- **Hold a gem show.** Each household publishes a digest, and a hub at the house gathers them into a family view with ribbons: most species, most elements on the periodic table, the full Mohs scale, the farthest find, find of the month.

## How identification works

**There is no Pl@ntNet for rocks.** The Aviary has BirdNET and the Conservatory has Pl@ntNet, classifiers trained on millions of labeled examples and free for a family to use. Nothing like that exists for minerals. The identifier is a general **vision language model**, held to a JSON schema, which returns the top two or three candidates with a confidence, the distinguishing features it saw in the photo, and the at-home tests that would separate its own candidates: a hardness scratch against glass, a streak on the back of a bathroom tile, a drop of vinegar for fizz, a fridge magnet. The Conservatory considered a vision model for plants and rejected it because a better tool existed. Here, it is the best tool there is.

**Two hosted models, not a local one.** The identifier is Claude Haiku, with Gemini Flash as a second opinion from a different company. Phase 0 measured them at under a tenth of a cent and about a third of a cent per photo (Gemini at low effort, which cost it no accuracy), so a family's whole backlog costs a dollar or two to identify. Both are more accurate than anything that runs locally, and hosted models mean every station identifies on its own, with nothing depending on the house, which keeps the Conservatory's rule. They are reached through the Vercel AI Gateway, the route the Aviary and the Conservatory already use: one key on a station reaches every provider's models by slug, at the provider's list price, and the gateway reports what each call cost. The models sit behind one interface (photos and notes in, candidates and tests out), so swapping either is a one-line configuration change, not a rewrite. Ollama on bluejay, once the fallback in case hosted models proved expensive, is not needed.

**The notes matter as much as the photo.** A mineral is defined by its chemistry and its crystal structure, and a photo shows neither. So the prompt carries everything the person can add: where it was found or bought, and the tests, each answered yes, no, or *not tried*, which is not the same as no. A photo gives no size on its own either, and for a fossil size is often the whole question, so the models are told to measure against a coin or ruler beside the stone (a US penny is 19 mm), never the framing, and to say "no scale in the photos" rather than guess. A vision model can also read, unlike a classifier, so a photo of the dealer's label or the gem show tag goes in as evidence too.

**Two opinions, in plain words.** A photo alone does not name a stone reliably. In Phase 0, on photos of 38 expert-identified specimens, the first guess was right about three times in five whichever of four models made it, and the dearest model did no better than the cheapest. What held up was agreement. When Claude Haiku and Gemini Flash, from different companies, named the same thing, they were right nearly nine times in ten, more often than two models from the same company that agreed. A model's own stated confidence helped less: Claude's 0.7 or more was right 28 times in 29 but came on only a third of the stones, and a low score marked a guess that changed when the same photo was asked again. So the confirm screen speaks in words, not percentages: **probably** galena when the two agree, **could be** pyrite or chalcopyrite when they don't. About one stone in six was beyond every model, the right name missing even from its top three, so "could be" never promises the answer is among the names it shows. Under each pill, "Why?" lists the features the models saw, never a confidence figure.

**The tests settle what the photo cannot, for whoever wants to be sure.** When the candidates are lookalikes, the confirm screen offers the test that separates them. A clear crystal could be quartz, calcite, or fluorite: quartz scratches glass, calcite fizzes in vinegar, fluorite does neither. A test is suggested only when the candidates would answer it differently, and each outcome is worded by candidate, so a person can tell which pill a result supports. Answering sends the job back through the models with the new facts. Nobody has to: the app names what it can without them, and the test waits behind "want to be sure?" This is the Quarry's version of the Conservatory's "add another photo of this plant" button.

**The owner confirms, and the app records how.** The models only suggest, and the owner may know no more than they do. The confirm screen shows the candidates as pills, a name field with autocomplete against the minerals already in the collection, and the options to skip for now or mark the photo as not a rock. Confirming saves at once, and a "Change" link reverses it. Both are kept, the models' candidates and the name the person confirmed, along with how it was confirmed: from a label, by the tests, by someone who knows (a rock club, a dealer), or by accepting the suggestion. Only the first three count as known answers. Over time the household builds its own accuracy record from them, and the next model can be scored against it before it is swapped in, without the app ever being graded on its own guesses.

**What a photo cannot show.** Lookalikes are the rule, not the exception: quartz, calcite, and fluorite; pyrite, marcasite, and chalcopyrite; red jasper and carnelian. Tumbled and polished stones have lost the crystal shape that would have told them apart. Much of a family collection is also not a mineral species. Granite, basalt, and obsidian are rocks, which are mixtures of minerals. Amethyst and agate are varieties of something else (quartz, chalcedony). A Petoskey stone is a fossil coral, made of calcite. Shop stones carry trade names like "Tiger's Eye", "Blue Lace Agate", and "Rainbow Moonstone", and are sometimes dyed, heat-treated, or lab-grown. The design consequences:

- A specimen is named the way a dealer's label at a rock show names it: the main mineral first, the others "with" it, and the base it grew "on" (Fluorite with Pyrite on Quartz; Amethyst on matrix, when the rock underneath has no name). Each mineral reads by its hobby or trade name when it has one (Amethyst, "Blue Lace Agate"), with the species it belongs to on the line under it ("A kind of Quartz"). Underneath, each mineral is still stored in two parts, the mineral or rock and an optional variety or trade name, and the Mindat record attaches to the first. This is the Conservatory's cultivar field, for stones.
- A fossil, a shell, or sea glass is named for what it is (Fossil coral, Petoskey stone), and what it is made of or preserved as (Calcite) is a field of its own. Two readings of the same fossil agree whatever each says it is made of.
- The form, as a rock hound bins a piece at a show (crystal, cluster, geode, druzy, rough, tumbled, slab, carved), is a tag beside the name, never part of it, and never a reason for two opinions to differ: a tumbled agate and a rough one are the same stone.
- A rock's property card is nearly empty, because Mindat records a type and little else for a rock. The page says so, with the info mark that explains what a rock is, and the stone's own notes and tests carry the rest.

**Rejected:** hobbyist mineral classifiers on Hugging Face (too few classes, and overconfident about the ones they have), mineralML (it needs oxide chemistry as input, not photos), and the consumer "Rock Identifier" apps (no API).

## Two ways to add a specimen, one queue

The best finds happen where there is no signal: a beach, a creek bed, a road cut, the far end of a gem mine. Both ways in use the same pipeline. The only difference is whether the person waits for the answer.

**From the camera roll, later.** Take photos at the beach with the normal camera app, which needs no signal: one close-up in daylight and one with a coin beside it for scale. Back home, open the Quarry on the house Wi-Fi and send them one rock at a time: choose that rock's photos (up to five), send it, and go on to the next without leaving the page. Each rock, with all its photos, becomes one queued job, so the models see the close-up, the scale shot and the label together. Come back whenever: the dashboard reserves a strip that reads "3 rocks waiting for a name", with Jasper's guess for the first, and tapping it walks through them one stone per screen, with large pills and the suggested tests. A photo left out of a rock's set goes on with "Add another photo of this stone" there. The camera roll is the offline mode. No service worker, no browser-side queue, nothing to break on an old phone.

**On the spot.** Take the photos in the app at the kitchen table. Sending the rock creates the same job, and the strip at the top of the add page shows Jasper at work on it, then turns amber a few seconds later with his guess and "Name it", which opens the confirm screen. Put the next rock together meanwhile, or walk away: the job simply waits in the dashboard's strip.

**The queue is the resilience.** Jobs live in SQLite on the station and survive a restart. A worker drains them one at a time, which keeps the model calls under any rate limit and makes a dropped connection or a provider outage a retry rather than a loss. Photos are shrunk in the browser to at most 2048 px on the long side before upload (an iPhone's HEIC is decoded in the page on a desktop browser that cannot draw one), each sent as its own request with retry, so a drop on the third photo keeps the first two, and a rock's job is made only once all of its photos are in. Jasper keeps every stored photo a JPEG within that size whatever arrives, and the models see a 1280 px copy he makes on the box. The queue matters more here than in the Conservatory, because the Quarry's first job is the backlog: a collection that already fills drawers arrives in batches of dozens.

Access from outside the house is not part of the design. The Quarry answers on the household's own Wi-Fi, and that covers every flow above.

## Enrichment

Jasper is the only process that calls Mindat or Wikipedia, as he is the only one that calls a model. A job's lookups are held to a few seconds so they never keep a person waiting; everything else runs between jobs, while the queue is empty, once per name. The web app reads only what he stored, so a stone's page keeps working when either service is down.

- **Mindat** is the field guide. Through the OpenMindat API (plain HTTP, as the gateway call is, with a free API key that Mindat approves by hand), Jasper looks up every candidate the models name and walks its chain of varieties up to the species (Crazy Lace Agate, Agate, Chalcedony, Quartz), which is how the confirm screen tells two names for one stone from a real disagreement. For each mineral the household names, he fetches the whole record once: formula, elements, Mohs hardness, crystal system, luster, streak, cleavage, specific gravity, fluorescence, and class. And he counts where Mindat reports it, in the USA, each state, and each of Michigan's counties, again each month. Every record is cached in the database, so a second specimen of the same mineral never re-queries.
- **Wikipedia** supplies the summary: the lead paragraphs of the most specific name with an article, so an Amethyst reads Amethyst's, not Quartz's. The reference image waits for the mineral pages; on a stone's page the household's own photos are the gallery.
- **A language model**, the same hosted one, will write short handling notes once per mineral from the Mindat record and store them: water-soluble (don't rinse the halite), fades in sunlight (keep the amethyst off the windowsill), wash your hands after handling (the realgar). Every figure the notes rely on is kept so it can be checked. Not built yet.

The household's own tests are checked against Mindat. On the confirm screen, a pill that cannot fit an answered scratch test already carries a quiet note ("Doesn't fit your glass test: Agate is Mohs 6.5 to 7"), and keeps its place, since a home test can be wrong. The stone page's version is to come: a "calcite" that someone recorded as scratching glass gets a note that calcite is Mohs 3, so either the name or the test is wrong. It is a comparison, not a model call, and it teaches the household how to read the card.

## Where it runs

On the same small boxes that run the Aviary and the Conservatory, one per household, alongside them. Each station has its own worker, database, and dashboard, calls the hosted models, Mindat, and Wikipedia directly, and never depends on the house. The code is a separate repository on purpose.

Today that is **orchid**, the house's box: the web app, Jasper, and a deploy watcher as systemd units, the database and photos outside the checkout, and a Caddy front door at `https://quarry.lan`. Merging a pull request is the deploy: the watcher pulls main within a minute and rebuilds what changed, and a web build that fails leaves the last one serving. How to bring it up, check it, back it up, and roll it back is in the [ops guide](docs/guide/ops.md).

The field stations at family members' houses come next, on the Aviary's pattern ([#100](https://github.com/reclinerhead/quarry/issues/100), [#101](https://github.com/reclinerhead/quarry/issues/101), [#102](https://github.com/reclinerhead/quarry/issues/102)): the Quarry under `/quarry` beside the Aviary on the station's one address, a release channel promoted nightly so a station takes main a day behind the house, and a front door at `quarry.toddtech.llc/<station>` so a bookmark survives the station's address changing. Tailscale between stations will let Phase 4's hub pull digests.

A hosted Quarry, on Vercel with Supabase, was weighed against the stations: it would serve the rock club and households without a box, and give printed labels a public address. It is tabled for its monthly cost ([#98](https://github.com/reclinerhead/quarry/issues/98)).

## Plan

- **Phase 0, the spike.** Done. A throwaway script (`spikes/vision/`) scored four hosted models on 38 expert-identified specimen photos from Wikimedia Commons, and on 11 of the family's own unnamed stones, for accuracy, calibration, agreement between models, and cost. It picked Claude Haiku with Gemini Flash as the second opinion; the numbers are in [#1](https://github.com/reclinerhead/quarry/issues/1). Mindat was checked against the same set, and the common shop stones, in [#21](https://github.com/reclinerhead/quarry/issues/21): it has every property card cell for nearly every mineral, little for a variety, and nothing but the name for a rock.
- **Phase 1, one household.** Built, and running at the house: capture from the camera roll and on the spot, the job queue, the confirm screen with its tests, the collection and the stone page, in the look `design/DESIGN.md` sets ([#4](https://github.com/reclinerhead/quarry/issues/4)). ## Open questions

- What the hub is called. The Aviary has Earl and Willard, and the Conservatory has Fern; the Quarry's worker is **Jasper**, a stone and an old-fashioned name, as Fern is a plant and one. The hub is provisionally **the Gem Show**.
- When to share code. The Quarry copied the Conservatory's uploader, queue, gateway client, and confirm screen rather than sharing them. A shared package waits until both apps have shipped and the seams are visible.
- Whether the find site can be read from a photo's location data before the browser's resize strips it. For now it is typed.
- Whether stations share one gateway key or each carries its own. Orchid's install offers either; the stations' plan ([#101](https://github.com/reclinerhead/quarry/issues/101)) gives each its own key and spend limit, so a runaway on one box cannot spend another's budget.
- Whether the Quarry should be hosted rather than run on the boxes ([#98](https://github.com/reclinerhead/quarry/issues/98), tabled).

Settled: the worker is Jasper, and Phase 1 copies from the Conservatory rather than sharing with it ([#4](https://github.com/reclinerhead/quarry/issues/4)). A specimen that is several minerals at once, such as calcite crystals on a bed of sphalerite, is a list of minerals, each with its own Mindat record, named as a show label names it ([#29](https://github.com/reclinerhead/quarry/issues/29)).
